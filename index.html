What pitch was that?

A model that names a pitch from its speed, spin, movement and spin direction. It learned from every MLB pitch of the 2024 and 2025 seasons, and it was tested on pitchers it never saw.

    Why I started this project

    Every bullpen I throw at Clarion is followed by a Rapsodo report, and for a long time I did not know what most of the numbers meant. A curveball was just a curveball, and a fastball was just a fastball. That changed when we got a new pitching coach who taught us what the numbers mean and how to use them to get more out of our pitches. Spin, break and spin direction became the way we talked about whether a pitch was working. Once I understood that, I started to wonder if I could train a model to tell what pitch was thrown from the numbers alone. Using only speed, spin and movement, could it tell a slider from a cutter or a changeup from a splitter? This project was my chance to find out. I also wanted to test it on college pitchers like me, not only on big leaguers.

    How it was built

    1. Data. Every pitch from the 2024 and 2025 MLB regular seasons, pulled from Baseball Savant with the pybaseball library. Pitchers with fewer than 100 pitches in a season were left out, as were rare pitch types like knuckleballs and eephus pitches.
    2. Features. Velocity, spin rate, induced vertical break, horizontal break and spin direction, plus how far each pitch sits from the pitcher's own fastball (the median of that pitcher's hardest 15% of pitches). Lefties are mirrored so one model reads both sides.
    3. Model. A gradient-boosted tree model (LightGBM) with 8 pitch types. It runs in your browser, so nothing you type is sent anywhere.
    4. Honest testing. Scores come from 5-fold cross-validation split by pitcher, so the model is always graded on pitchers it never trained on. When it sees a whole outing, each pitch also votes with the 10 most similar pitches from the same pitcher.
    5. College test. The finished model, unchanged, was run on TrackMan data from college exhibition games, where a trained operator tagged every pitch by hand. Those tags use 5 broader groups, so the model's calls were folded into the same groups (a sinker counts as a fastball, a sweeper as a slider).

    College data: Pifer, N. D., Optical Tracking Data from College Baseball Scrimmages, Mendeley Data, V3, doi:10.17632/xfnz6mkdzm.3, licensed CC BY 4.0. Changes made: pitch types grouped as described above. MLB data from Baseball Savant.
